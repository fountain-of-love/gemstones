# Diamond: Characteristics and Effects

## Overview

Diamond is crystalline carbon: transparent or opaque, naturally colorless through yellow, brown, blue, green, pink, red, and black, and the hardest natural mineral in the Mohs scale. Its reputation comes from the combination of hardness, dispersion, and the way faceting turns white light into spectral fire.

The [[#Composition|composition]] is chemically simple but visually variable: C + trace elements and inclusions. Nitrogen commonly contributes yellow color, boron can contribute blue, manganese can contribute pink or red, and graphite or carbon-rich inclusions can make material black.

Diamond also tests the difference between material and marketing. “Brilliant” is a cut style, not a mineral species, while many historic trade names use “diamond” for quartz, hematite, zircon, or topaz imitations. Industrial diamond is used for cutting, grinding, polishing, saws, drills, and tools.

## Geology And Occurrence

Most natural diamonds formed deep in the mantle, commonly around 150–250 km below the surface, under high pressure and temperature. Volcanic eruptions later carried them upward in kimberlite and related host rocks. Natural diamonds range from tens of millions to billions of years old.

## History And Cultural Use

Diamond has long been used in seals, royal regalia, wedding jewelry, crowns, tiaras, and investment stones. The source connects the modern engagement-ring tradition with De Beers' 1947 “A diamond is forever” campaign and notes that diamonds are national stones of India, the Netherlands, and South Africa.

The material's hardness has also made it an industrial tool. Diamond powder, boart, and low-quality stones are used in cutting, drilling, sawing, grinding, polishing, and other abrasive applications.

## Did You Know

- Diamond is about 140 times more abrasion-resistant than corundum, although hardness and toughness are different properties.
- The largest gem-quality stones are rare; much mined diamond is industrial material.
- Diamond and graphite are both carbon but have different crystal structures and properties.
- High dispersion is responsible for the rainbow “fire” of a well-cut diamond.
