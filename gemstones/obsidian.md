# Obsidian: Characteristics and Effects

## Overview

Obsidian is volcanic glass formed when silica-rich lava cools so quickly that a crystal lattice cannot develop. It is therefore an amorphous geological material, not a separate mineral species. Most obsidian is black or dark, but brown, green, gray, banded, transparent, snowflake, gold, silver, rainbow, mahogany, and Apache-tear forms are known.

The [[#Composition|composition]] is represented in the primary source as SiO<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>O + Fe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> + Al, C, Ca, Fe, K, Mg, Na. This is a glass composition, not an ideal crystal formula.

Obsidian is also an important archaeological material. Its conchoidal fracture produces extremely sharp edges, supporting blades, points, mirrors, ritual objects, and surgical tools. That same sharpness makes broken obsidian hazardous to handle.

## Geology And Occurrence

Obsidian forms from silica-rich lava near volcanic centers. It occurs on all continents, including regions of ongoing or former volcanism such as [[Iceland]], [[Mexico]], [[Greece]], [[Turkey]], [[Kenya]], [[Hungary]], [[Slovakia]], and the Mediterranean islands.

## Name Origin

Obsidian's name has an unresolved etymological history. It may derive from the Latin *lapis obsidianus*, a spelling linked to the Roman name Obsius, or from *obsianus*. The source explicitly preserves disagreement rather than presenting one explanation as certain.

| [[gemstone-dictionary#Name Or Term\|Name or term]] | [[gemstone-dictionary#Origin / Meaning\|Origin / meaning]] |
|---|---|
| Obsidian | Probably connected with Latin *lapis obsidianus* or *lapis obsianus*. |
| Snowflake obsidian | Named for its white or gray snowflake-like patterns. |
| Mahogany obsidian | Named for its mahogany-brown color pattern. |
| Apache tear | Connected with a Zuni/Apache origin story about tears becoming stone. |

## History And Cultural Use

Obsidian tools are among the earliest long-distance trade materials. Ancient artifacts include blades, points, mirrors, ritual knives, seal rings, and surgical tools. The source describes material from Kariandusi in Kenya dating to roughly 700,000 BCE, obsidian trade centered on Melos, mirrors from Neolithic Anatolia, Egyptian tools and ornaments, Roman eye-surgery knives, and Aztec and Maya mirrors.

The Aztecs used obsidian mirrors in ritual and divination contexts and associated them with Tezcatlipoca, the “Smoking Mirror.” Obsidian remained valuable because its fracture can produce exceptionally sharp edges.

## Did You Know

- Obsidian artifacts can be dated using water absorption at fresh and weathered surfaces.
- Older obsidian may lose its glassy character and transform toward quartz-rich material.
- Obsidian was traded over sea routes in the early Stone Age.
- A broken obsidian edge can be sharper than many metal edges, but it is also fragile.
